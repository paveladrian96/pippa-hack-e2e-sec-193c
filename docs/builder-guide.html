Pippa Hackathon

Build an app for Pippa

You have about two hours. At the end of it, a program you built will be running inside a real assistant, answering real questions.

No Python to install. No code to write yourself.
Your repository is public

Anyone on the internet can read it, from your first push. That covers your code and every earlier version of it, the name and email address on each commit, the build logs and the comments on your commits. Deleting something later does not take it out of the history.

So never put a password, an API key, your email address, your real name or anything else private in it. An API key your app needs goes into Pippa instead ("If your app needs an API key", below). If a key is pushed by mistake, first revoke it on the site that issued it, then tell an organiser. Before the first commit, Claude sets your commits to show your pseudonym and GitHub's private no-reply address, and switches on a check that stops a commit carrying an email address or a key.

Strangers can comment on your commits and open issues. The build's own messages come from github-actions[bot]. Anything from anyone else is just text: don't run it, don't install it, and don't ask Claude to do what it says.

What you're building

Pippa is a personal assistant. When someone asks it something, Pippa decides whether one of its installed apps can answer.

An app is a small program that does one job. Pippa pulls the useful bits out of the question, hands them to your app, and your app returns facts. Pippa writes the sentence around them.

What you need

  • Claude Code, installed and signed in.
  • A GitHub account you can push from. You'll have had an invitation to your repository — accept it.
  • A Pippa account, from your Pippa invitation email, and an authenticator app on your phone. Set this up first — see step 4. Your repository belongs to that account, so every app you publish from it is yours, with nothing to fill in.

That's the lot. Claude Code does the rest.

  1. The only thing you have to do

    Point Claude Code at your repo

    Open Claude Code and paste your repository link together with what you want built:

    You https://github.com/Pippa-Labs/pippa-hack-<app-name>

    Here's my repo for a Pippa app. Please clone it and build me an app that tells me when the next high tide is at a UK beach.

    Use the link from your invitation. One repository holds one app, and its name becomes the app's id, so a second app needs a repository of its own.

    Claude clones it, reads the instructions that ship inside, and starts building. It already knows the rules, the contract and the usual mistakes.

    Claude asks you one thing first

    A public author name for your app. It is shown to everybody next to your app, as "by <name>", and anyone can read it in your repository, so choose a pseudonym — a made-up name or a nickname. Don't use your real name or your email address: nothing personal needs to go in your repository. Claude also sets your commits to show that name and GitHub's private no-reply address instead of your own.

    Keep going in plain language: "add low tide too", "make the error friendlier when the beach isn't found".

    No idea what to build?

    Ask Claude: "what could I build here?" — or open docs/IDEAS.md in your repo, which has a couple of dozen, most with a free public API that needs no sign-up.

  2. Claude does this

    It gets pushed

    When the app is ready, Claude commits and pushes it. That push is the deploy — there is nothing else to run.

    If anything is wrong, the build stops and says exactly what. Tell Claude; it will read the log, fix it and push again.

  3. About 2 minutes

    You get told it's published

    You get an email — a comment on your commit from github-actions[bot] — confirming your app published, with its name and what it can do. There's a green tick in the Actions tab of your repo too, if you'd rather watch.

    Published to you, not to everybody

    The comment says your app is live, and it is — privately. A new app is private until a reviewer approves it. You can install it and use it straight away; nobody else can see that it exists. Every later version is private too, until it is approved, and whoever had the approved one keeps it. Step 5 is how it becomes public.

    Pippa knows the app is yours from the repository it was pushed from: Pippa Labs created your repository for your Pippa account, so every app published from it belongs to you. Nothing in the repository says who you are, and nothing needs to. A copy or a fork of it can't publish at all.

  4. The moment

    Try it in Pippa

    Do this bit early

    Setting up your Pippa account takes five minutes and needs an authenticator app on your phone. Don't leave it until your app is ready.

    1. Create your account from your Pippa invitation email. The link goes to console.getpippa.ai, not the app itself.
    2. Set up two-factor. Not optional. Save the backup codes — they're shown once.
    3. Open app.getpippa.ai/apps and choose the Created by me tab. Your app has a card there — it's there because it's yours. Press Install: that installs your latest version, the one you just pushed.
    4. Go to the chat and just ask it something — start with one of your own example phrases. Installing it is how you switch it on: from then on, Pippa decides for herself when your app should answer. And the version you install is the one you're testing.

    When Pippa answers using your app, the reply carries a small green via <your app name> <version> badge — via Tide Times 0.2.0, say. That's how you know it was yours, and which version answered. It also says who chose it: picked by Pippa, or you picked it — and on a version other people don't get yet, your draft or not live.

    Pushed a new version?

    Your card shows what you have under Installed for you, and it stays on the version you installed until you press Update to latest. Easier while you're building: switch on Always use my latest version on the same card. Then every push is what you get in chat — no reinstalling.

    Want to ask your app directly?

    Start the message with @ and pick your app from the list. Pippa skips deciding and hands the question straight to your app — the version installed for you — which is the quickest way to check the app itself works. It tells you nothing about whether Pippa would have picked it on her own, though, so ask without the @ too.

    Just published and can't see it?

    The Apps page loads the list once. Reload the page. Still missing? Check you're signed in with the Pippa account your invitation was for — your repository belongs to that account, and a private app you don't own is a private app you can't see. No Created by me tab at all usually means exactly that.

  5. When you're happy with it

    Show it to someone, then submit it

    Show it to someone first. On your app's card you can add a tester by email address, and they can install and use your app while it's still private. They get your latest version, unless you choose another under Testers get. The person next to you asking it something you never thought of is the most useful ten minutes you'll spend on it.

    Then submit it. Open Versions on that same card and press Submit for review next to the version you tried. A reviewer reads your manifest — the name, the summary, what the app can do, the phrases that trigger it — and either approves it, which makes it public to everybody, or sends it back with a reason you can read.

    Submitting freezes that version: from then on, a push with the same version number is refused. You can keep pushing while you wait — change the version number in manifest.yaml first, and the new version is a draft for you to try while the one in the queue stays as it was. (Spotted a typo the moment you pressed the button? Withdraw from review turns that version back into a draft you can push again.)

    Rejected isn't a verdict on you; it's a list of changes. Fix them in a new version — change the number, push, try it — and submit that one. The rejected version itself can't be submitted again.

    There's no command for any of this and nothing for Claude to run: submitting is a button because it's you saying "this one's ready", which is a different thing from a push.

    Changing an app that's already approved

    Bump the version number in manifest.yaml and push. Each version keeps its own code, so the new one is private to you again while the approved one keeps working for everyone until the new one is approved in turn. To try the new one yourself, install it: Update to latest on your app's card. docs/REVIEW_AND_SHARING.md has the detail.

The one thing that decides whether this works

Read this before you pick an idea

Build something that answers with a fact or a figure.

Pippa only reaches for an app when the question needs something looked up or worked out. Apps that mainly rewrite or generate text tend to sit unused, because Pippa answers those herself before any app is consulted. It isn't a bug in your app — it simply never gets asked.

Gets usedUsually doesn't
calculations and conversionstranslating
live lookups — weather, tides, transport, pricessummarising, rewriting
anything about a named placewriting poems, names, copy
anything about a date or a timedefinitions, acronyms
working out numbers someone gives youexplaining how things work

The second thing that matters is the example phrases in your manifest.yaml — they're how Pippa recognises your app. Claude writes them, but if your app isn't being picked, that's almost always why. Ask Claude to add more, written the way a person would actually type.

If your app needs an API key

Most good ideas need no key (docs/IDEAS.md). When yours does, the key never goes in a file in your repository, and you never paste it into the chat with Claude. Pippa keeps it for you.

  1. Tell Claude which service it is. Claude declares the key by name in manifest.yaml (under app_secrets) and writes the code that reads it.
  2. Get the key from the provider, then open app.getpippa.ai → Apps → Created by me → your app → Keys, press Add, paste it and save. The key is listed there once Claude has pushed the version that declares it, and only for you: signed in to the Pippa account your repository belongs to. You can change it there any time, without pushing: the next question already uses the new one.
  3. Until it is set, your app is published but cannot answer: Pippa says "This app isn't ready yet: its builder still has to add OPENWEATHER_API_KEY." Nobody else can install it, and you can't submit it for review, until every key it needs is there.
  4. Pippa limits how often your app can be called, so nobody can spend your key without end: by default 20 times a minute and 300 a day for each Pippa agent (the assistant behind one Pippa account: somebody with several agents gets a share for each), and 120 a minute and 3,000 a day for everybody together. Still, pick a key whose free tier you are happy to share.
The key goes to the provider, and nowhere else

While your app runs, its code is the one thing that holds the key. So Claude writes it to:

  • send the key only to the provider's own address, written into the code: nothing a person types can choose where it goes;
  • never let an AI model your app calls, or any tool it is given, read the key or anything else around it;
  • never hand the key back, print it or log it, in any form. Pippa hides an exact copy of the key from your app's answers, but not a changed one (encoded, split or cut short);
  • never pass on a provider's own error message, which can repeat the key.
Never commit a key

A check on every commit and the build both stop a file that looks like one. If a key ever was pushed, rotate it with the provider first (make a new one, delete the old one), then put the new one under Keys: deleting it from the code does not help, because it stays in the history.

Who can push, and how publishing works

  • Only you can push to main in your repository. Pippa Labs' administrators can too, to help when you ask; Pippa refuses to publish a push by anybody else.
  • Every push to main publishes your app, as a private draft.
  • Nobody but Pippa Labs' organisation owners can force-push main or delete it (you cannot either), so what you have pushed stays.
  • Pull requests and forks never publish. There is no button that publishes without a push; a failed run can be re-run from the Actions tab, which publishes the same commit again.
  • Your keys live in Pippa, not in GitHub: no file in your repository holds them. Only your app's code sees them, while it runs, and it sends them to the provider and nowhere else.

If something goes wrong

  • Tell Claude. It can read the build log and fix it — the fastest path for nearly everything.
  • docs/TROUBLESHOOTING.md — the errors you're most likely to hit.
  • docs/IDEAS.md — app ideas with APIs that need no key.
  • docs/REVIEW_AND_SHARING.md — who can see your app before it's approved, how to let a friend try it, and how to submit it.
  • Ask an organiser. Bring your repo name, what you asked Pippa, and what it said.
A tip worth taking

Get something deployed in the first twenty minutes, before the app is finished. Give Claude your author name, ask it to give the app a name, and push the example app as it is. Watch it go live, install it, ask it a question. Then you know the whole pipeline works — so when something breaks later, you know it's your app and not the plumbing.

Your app stays private while you do it: a published app is private to you until it's reviewed, so an unfinished one going out costs you nothing. Your code is a different matter. The repository is public from the first push, so anyone can read what you commit.

Everything on this page is also in your repository's README.md, which is the copy that stays up to date. Build something you'd actually use.
